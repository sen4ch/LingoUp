There is an old fable about an empire whose mapmakers grew so ambitious that they produced a map on the scale of one mile to the mile. It was, of course, perfectly accurate and perfectly useless: unrolled across the provinces, it covered the very land it was meant to describe, and later generations abandoned it to the wind and the weather. The story endures because it captures a truth that every cartographer, scientist and teacher eventually confronts — a representation is valuable precisely because of what it leaves out.

Every map is an exercise in abstraction. A road atlas ignores the soil; a geological survey ignores the roads. Each makes a tacit claim about what matters, and the confident lines on the page belie the thousands of judgements behind them. Where exactly does a coastline lie when the tide moves twice a day? Which of two rival names should a disputed river carry? The map answers these questions silently, and its very clarity discourages us from asking them.

The danger, then, is not simplification itself but forgetting that simplification has taken place. When a model acquires enough verisimilitude, we begin to treat it as the territory rather than a sketch of it. Economists mistake their equations for markets; learners mistake a grammar table for the living language, whose rules are inextricably bound up with context, tone and habit. The table is indispensable, yet no one has ever become fluent by memorising it alone.

To believe that a sufficiently detailed model would eliminate this gap is a kind of hubris — the same impulse that produced the empire's useless map. A better attitude might be a cheerful humility: to use maps constantly, to draw new ones whenever the old ones mislead us, and to remember, each time we unfold one, that the world it depicts is larger, stranger and more interesting than any picture of it could be.
