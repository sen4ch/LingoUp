For most of the twentieth century, the five-day, forty-hour week was considered the natural shape of working life. Today a growing number of companies are questioning that assumption by moving to four days of work for the same salary. Supporters claim it makes employees happier without hurting the business; critics argue that it simply cannot work everywhere.

The case in favour rests largely on productivity. Several organisations that ran trials reported that output stayed roughly the same, while sick days and staff turnover fell. The explanation usually offered is that people with a long weekend arrive more rested, waste less time in unnecessary meetings and plan their work more carefully. Reducing burnout, supporters say, is not just kind — it is good business, because replacing an experienced employee is expensive.

Critics point out that these trials tend to involve office jobs where tasks can be reorganised. A hospital, a school or a restaurant cannot simply close on Fridays; it would need to hire more staff to cover the same hours, which raises costs considerably. There is also a risk that the workload does not shrink but is squeezed into fewer days, leaving people more stressed than before.

In practice, the results seem to depend on how the change is introduced. Companies that treated the shorter week as an opportunity to rethink how they work — cutting low-value tasks, setting clear priorities — tended to succeed. Those that just removed a day and hoped for the best often went back to the old system.

Perhaps the real lesson is that there is no universal answer. Every model involves a trade-off, and what works for a software company may fail in a factory. Still, the experiment has forced many employers to ask a useful question: are we measuring the hours people spend at work, or the value they create? In the long run, that question may matter more than the number of days in the week.
