When I was a child, my family lived on the eighth floor of a building next to a busy road. There was nowhere safe to ride a bike, so I never learnt. For years this didn't matter. Then I moved to a city where everybody seemed to cycle — to work, to school, even to the supermarket.

At first I was embarrassed. How do you tell your colleagues that you, a thirty-five-year-old engineer, can't ride a bicycle? I made excuses: the weather was bad, my bag was too heavy, I preferred walking. In the end, a friend found an evening class for adults, and I signed up before I could change my mind.

The first lesson was terrible. We didn't even use the pedals. The teacher told us to sit on the bike and push the ground with our feet, like children on a balance bike. I felt ridiculous, and twice I nearly gave up. But slowly I started to understand what balance actually feels like: you don't stay still, you keep making tiny corrections.

By the fifth week I could ride around the car park without stopping. By the eighth week I cycled to the station. The confidence I got from those lessons surprised me. If I could learn this, what else could I learn?

Today my commute takes twenty minutes instead of fifty, and I look forward to it every morning. More importantly, I have stopped saying "I'm too old for that."
