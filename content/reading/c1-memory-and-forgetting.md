In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus spent years memorising lists of meaningless syllables and testing how quickly he forgot them. The result, now known as the forgetting curve, is sobering: without review, a large share of new information fades within days. Yet the most interesting part of his work is not how fast we forget, but what happens when we try to remember.

It sounds counterintuitive, but forgetting is not simply the enemy of learning. When a memory has started to fade and we make the effort to retrieve it, that act of retrieval strengthens it far more than rereading the same material would. Psychologists call this a "desirable difficulty": the struggle itself is what signals to the brain that the information is worth keeping.

This explains why cramming is so ineffective in the long term. Reading the same notes five times in one evening produces a comforting feeling of familiarity, but the information is still fresh, so each repetition requires almost no effort and quickly reaches diminishing returns. Spread the same five sessions over two weeks, and each one demands genuine recall. Retention a month later can be dramatically higher.

Spaced repetition software turns this principle into an algorithm. Each time you review an item, the program estimates how likely you are to remember it and schedules the next review for the moment just before you would probably forget. Easy items drift further and further apart; difficult ones come back sooner. Modern schedulers model not just the interval but the stability and difficulty of each individual memory.

By the same token, the experience of using such a system can feel frustrating. You are constantly asked about things you are not quite sure of, which is precisely the point. The feeling of struggling is not a sign that you are failing to learn — it is often the clearest sign that you are learning.
