## Meaning and use

English has present and past inflected tense forms, and several constructions for future reference. Teaching materials often organise twelve common combinations of time reference and aspect into a useful reference grid. Treat this as a map of patterns, not twelve isolated formulas with one automatic translation each. Simple forms can present habits or complete events; continuous forms present an activity from inside; perfect forms relate an earlier situation to a viewpoint.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Present: works; is working; has worked; has been working.
- Past: worked; was working; had worked; had been working.
- Future constructions: will work; will be working; will have worked; will have been working.
- A form's grammatical name does not determine every possible time reference.

## Examples in context

> I work here, but I am working from home this week.
> I had finished before the meeting began.
> By June, I will have been working here for a year.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not choose a tense only because a sentence contains one memorised signal word. Present forms can describe future timetables, and past forms can express hypothetical remoteness or politeness. State verbs often resist continuous readings. Will is a modal used for future reference, predictions, and other meanings, not a suffix added to an English verb.

## Present-reference patterns

- Present simple: I work — routines, facts, states, and timetables. Negative: I do not work. Question: Do you work?
- Present continuous: I am working — temporary or ongoing activity. Negative: I am not working. Question: Are you working?
- Present perfect: I have worked — experience, current relevance, or a continuing state. Negative: I have not worked. Question: Have you worked?
- Present perfect continuous: I have been working — activity or duration viewed from now. Negative: I have not been working. Question: Have you been working?

## Past-reference patterns

- Past simple: I worked — finished past events or states. Negative: I did not work. Question: Did you work?
- Past continuous: I was working — activity in progress at a past viewpoint. Negative: I was not working. Question: Were you working?
- Past perfect: I had worked — an earlier situation viewed from a past point. Negative: I had not worked. Question: Had you worked?
- Past perfect continuous: I had been working — earlier activity or duration viewed from a past point. Negative: I had not been working. Question: Had you been working?

## Future-reference constructions

- Will + base: I will work — a prediction, decision, promise, or other modal meaning. Negative: I will not work. Question: Will you work?
- Future continuous: I will be working — an activity viewed as ongoing at a future time. Negative: I will not be working. Question: Will you be working?
- Future perfect: I will have worked — an earlier situation viewed from a future point. Negative: I will not have worked. Question: Will you have worked?
- Future perfect continuous: I will have been working — duration of an activity up to a future point. Negative: I will not have been working. Question: Will you have been working?

## Auxiliary order

A useful order is modal, perfect have, continuous be, passive be, lexical verb. Each auxiliary determines the form that follows it. In must have been repaired, must is followed by base have; have by participle been; passive be by participle repaired. Long combinations are possible, but an alternative sentence is often clearer.
