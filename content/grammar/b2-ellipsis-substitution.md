## Meaning and use

English avoids repetition by leaving out recoverable material or replacing it with a shorter expression. Ellipsis omits words that the context supplies. Substitution uses forms such as one, do, so, or not. The missing or substituted material must be identifiable, and the replacement must match the grammatical structure. These devices are especially common in conversation, answers, and coordinated clauses.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- One/ones can replace a countable noun: the blue one, the smaller ones.
- Auxiliary ellipsis: I have finished, but she hasn't.
- So/neither + auxiliary + subject: So do I; Neither can we.
- I think so / I hope not can replace a proposition.

## Examples in context

> Would you like a ticket? I already have one.
> Maya can drive, and so can her brother.
> I wanted to call, but I did not have time to.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Choose the auxiliary that matches the original structure: I am tired—So am I, not So do I. One does not normally replace an uncountable noun in the same way: some fresh water, not a fresh one. Distinguish agreement So do I from confirmation So I do. Do not omit material when the reader would have to guess its identity.
