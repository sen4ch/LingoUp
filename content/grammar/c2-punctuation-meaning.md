## Meaning and use

Punctuation helps signal syntactic structure and intended interpretation, but it does not repair a fundamentally unclear sentence. Commas can distinguish defining and supplementary information, separate clauses, and mark parenthetical material. Colons, semicolons, dashes, and parentheses organise relationships at different levels. Their use varies somewhat by editorial style. Choose punctuation by analysing the clause or phrase it relates to.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- A semicolon can link closely related independent clauses.
- A colon can introduce an explanation or list after an appropriate lead-in.
- Paired commas or dashes can set off a parenthetical interruption.
- Apostrophes mark possession or omitted letters, not ordinary plurals.

## Examples in context

> The applicants who passed were contacted selects a subset.
> The applicants, who passed, were contacted presents the relative information as supplementary.
> One problem remained: the original file was missing.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

A comma splice joins independent clauses with only a comma in edited prose. Avoid inserting a comma between an ordinary subject and its verb simply because the subject is long. A serial comma can clarify a list but cannot solve every ambiguity. Direct-quotation punctuation differs between publishing conventions; keep grammatical completeness and the actual quoted words clear.
