## Meaning and use

The second conditional presents a situation as hypothetical, unlikely, or remote from reality in the present or future. A past form in the condition normally marks remoteness, not past time. The result often uses would, could, or might plus a base verb. Speakers choose this pattern to imagine alternatives, consider consequences, or give advice. It does not always mean an outcome is impossible.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- If + past form, would + base verb.
- Could expresses hypothetical ability; might expresses a possible result.
- If I were you is a common advice pattern.
- Were can appear with singular subjects in formal hypothetical clauses.

## Examples in context

> If I had more space, I would grow vegetables.
> If she lived nearer, we could meet more often.
> If I were you, I would check the contract.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not put would in an ordinary second-conditional if clause: If I knew, I would tell you. Informal was is common with I/he/she in some hypothetical contexts, while were remains a useful formal choice. Compare If I get the job with If I got the job: the difference is the speaker's presentation of the possibility, not a calculation of its probability.
