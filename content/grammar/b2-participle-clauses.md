## Meaning and use

A participle clause can express background, reason, time, or a result without a full finite clause. Present participles often have an active interpretation; past participles often have a passive interpretation. A perfect participle can emphasise an earlier completed action. In an ordinary introductory supplementary participle clause, the understood subject is normally the main-clause subject. Reduced relative descriptions are another useful non-finite pattern.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Active: Walking home, she noticed the new cafe.
- Passive: Built in 1890, the house needs repairs.
- Earlier action: Having finished the report, he left.
- Reduced relative: the people waiting outside; the documents attached to the email.

## Examples in context

> Feeling tired, I decided to take a break.
> Written in clear English, the guide is easy to follow.
> Having checked the figures, we sent the report.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Avoid an unintended dangling modifier: Walking home, the rain soaked my coat wrongly suggests that the rain was walking. A participle does not by itself identify tense; the main clause and context do. Some absolute constructions have their own subject, such as weather permitting, but these are different from the ordinary shared-subject teaching pattern.
