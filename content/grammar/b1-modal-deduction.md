## Meaning and use

Modal deductions express the speaker's assessment of evidence. Must can mark a strong positive conclusion; cannot/can't can mark a strong negative conclusion. May, might, and could can mark possibility rather than certainty. These meanings differ from obligation, permission, and ability. The surrounding words and situation tell you which interpretation applies. Modal deductions can refer to a state or an activity in progress.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Strong positive deduction: must + base verb.
- Strong negative deduction: cannot + base verb.
- Possibility: may/might/could + base verb.
- Ongoing activity: modal + be + verb-ing.

## Examples in context

> The lights are on. Someone must be at home.
> That cannot be Mia; she is abroad.
> He might be waiting at the other entrance.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not use must not as the normal opposite of deductive must. Must not often means prohibition; cannot gives the usual strong negative deduction. A deduction is an inference, not a guarantee of truth. Could is compatible with a possible explanation but does not normally express the same certainty as must. Past deductions need modal + have + past participle.
