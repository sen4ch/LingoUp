## Meaning and use

The present perfect continuous looks back from now at an activity continuing over a period or recently ending with visible consequences. It often emphasises duration, repetition, or the activity itself rather than a completed total. It combines have/has been with an -ing form. The simple perfect is often preferable for a completed result, a count, or a state. Some verbs allow both forms with a difference in emphasis.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Have/has been + verb-ing.
- Duration: for two hours; starting point: since lunchtime.
- Activity: I have been reading; total: I have read three chapters.
- Recent consequence: Your hands are dirty. Have you been gardening?

## Examples in context

> We have been waiting since eight.
> It has been raining, so the pavement is wet.
> She has written five pages and has been working all morning.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not normally use a continuous form with a state such as know: I have known her for years. The activity may have just stopped, so the continuous does not always imply it is still happening. Live and work often allow either perfect form. Use has with he/she/it, even though been and the main verb remain unchanged.
