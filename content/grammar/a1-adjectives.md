## Meaning and use

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun. It often comes before a noun or after a linking verb such as be, seem, or look. English adjectives normally do not change for plural nouns, and they do not take an indefinite article on their own. When several adjectives appear together, a familiar general order helps the phrase sound natural. Opinion often comes before physical description; material and purpose usually stay close to the noun.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Before a noun: a quiet street; after be: The street is quiet.
- No plural adjective ending: two quiet streets.
- A useful order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose, noun.
- Some adjectives prefer one position: the child is asleep; an elderly person.

## Examples in context

> We stayed in a lovely small wooden house.
> The soup smells delicious.
> Her new shoes are black.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not write reds cars: say red cars. Use an adjective after a linking verb to describe its subject: She looks happy. Use an adverb to describe how an action happens: She smiled happily. Long adjective strings are difficult to read; using a shorter phrase is often better than memorising a rigid list. The more advanced comparison lessons explain comparative and superlative forms.
