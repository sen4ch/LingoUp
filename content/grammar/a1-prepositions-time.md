## Meaning and use

Use time prepositions to locate an event. **At** is common with clock times, **on** with days and dates, and **in** with months, years, seasons, and longer periods. Some familiar expressions must be learned as whole phrases. A modifier such as next or last often removes the need for a preposition. These patterns describe when something happens; duration is a separate question answered by phrases such as for two hours.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- At six o’clock, at noon, at night.
- On Tuesday, on 14 May, on Friday morning.
- In April, in 2028, in winter, in the afternoon.
- No preposition before last, next, this, every: next week, every morning.

## Examples in context

> The meeting starts at half past ten.
> We moved here in September.
> I will call you on Monday afternoon.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Say **next Monday**, not on next Monday. A particular day part takes on when attached to a named day: on Saturday evening, but in the evening. British English commonly uses at the weekend; American English commonly uses on the weekend. In two hours normally means two hours from now; for two hours describes a duration. The choice depends on meaning, not just the number.
