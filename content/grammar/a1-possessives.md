## Meaning and use

Possessive forms show ownership and relationships. A **possessive determiner** comes before a noun; a **possessive pronoun** replaces the whole possessive noun phrase. Apostrophe s attaches to a noun phrase, often a person or animal, to show a relationship. Possession does not always mean physical ownership: my sister and the teacher’s idea show connections. The form its describes something related to a thing or animal; it is different from the contraction it’s.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Before a noun: my, your, his, her, its, our, their.
- Without a following noun: mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs.
- Singular possessor: the girl’s coat; regular plural possessor: the girls’ coats.
- Irregular plural possessor: the children’s toys.

## Examples in context

> This is my notebook. That one is yours.
> Our neighbours’ garden is very small.
> The dog hurt its paw.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not put an apostrophe in yours, hers, ours, or theirs. **It’s** means it is or it has; **its** is possessive. The position of an apostrophe changes meaning: a student’s books belong to one student, while students’ books belong to several. An of phrase is often natural for things: the roof of the house. Possessive s can also be used with time: a day’s work.
