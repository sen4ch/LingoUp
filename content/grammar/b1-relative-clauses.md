## Meaning and use

A defining relative clause tells the reader which person or thing you mean. It follows the noun phrase it modifies and normally has no surrounding commas. Who commonly refers to people, which to things, and that to either in defining clauses. A relative word can be the subject or object of its clause. When it is the object, it can often be omitted; a subject relative cannot simply disappear.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Person: the artist who painted this picture.
- Thing: the book that changed my mind.
- Object omission: the book I borrowed; not the book borrowed me.
- Whose + noun shows a relationship: the woman whose dog escaped.

## Examples in context

> The neighbour who lives upstairs is a musician.
> This is the camera that I use for work.
> I met a student whose parents live nearby.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not repeat the relative subject or object: the woman who works here, not the woman who she works here. Do not insert commas when the clause defines the intended group. Where can introduce a place relation, but a place noun can still take which when it is a direct object: the town which we visited.
