## Meaning and use

Written English uses punctuation and capitalisation to show sentence boundaries, relationships, and names. A full stop ends a statement; a question mark normally ends a direct question; an exclamation mark adds force. Commas separate certain phrases and clauses, while semicolons and colons connect or introduce larger units. Punctuation conventions can vary across publishers and varieties, so keep both grammatical structure and the required style in mind.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Capitalise sentence starts, I, proper names, languages, nationalities, weekdays and months.
- Do not normally put a comma between an ordinary subject and verb or verb and object.
- Use an apostrophe for possession or a contraction, not an ordinary plural.
- A semicolon can link independent clauses; a colon can introduce an explanation or list.

## Examples in context

> On Monday, Priya started learning Italian.
> We had one option: wait until morning.
> The result was unexpected; however, it was not impossible.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

A question word does not automatically require a question mark: I wonder where she lives is a statement. Avoid a comma splice between independent clauses. Hyphens help some compound modifiers, such as a three-year plan, but highly trained staff normally has no hyphen after the -ly adverb. Its and it's have different functions. Quotation conventions differ, so use one consistent system.

## Commas and clause boundaries

Use a comma after many introductory dependent clauses: When the lesson ended, we left. Supplementary material often needs paired commas: Our tutor, who lives nearby, cycles to work. Short introductory phrases do not always need a comma. A comma before a coordinating conjunction can help separate two independent clauses, especially when they are long.

## Colons, semicolons and dashes

A colon can announce an explanation, quotation, or list after a suitable lead-in. Avoid placing it casually between a verb and its object. A semicolon separates related independent clauses or complex list items. Dashes can set off an interruption or emphasised explanation; do not use them as a substitute for every other punctuation mark.

## Apostrophes and quotations

- One student's notes: one owner. Several students' notes: plural owners. Children's notes: an irregular plural owner.
- Contractions show omitted letters: do not/don't, she is/she's.
- Direct speech reproduces the speaker's wording; reported speech normally does not use quotation marks around the report.
- Quoted questions keep their question mark when the quoted words themselves are a question.

## Parentheses and hyphens

Parentheses add supplementary information. Keep the main sentence grammatical when the parenthetical material is removed. A full parenthetical sentence and a parenthetical phrase can require different full-stop placement. Compound-modifier hyphenation is partly conventional; consult a style guide for specialised terms and remain consistent.
