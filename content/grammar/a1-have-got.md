## Meaning and use

Have can express possession, relationships, and physical characteristics. **Have got** is another common present-time possession pattern, particularly in conversational British English. Have without got also forms expressions for experiences and activities, such as have lunch or have a shower. These activity uses are not freely replaceable by have got. Learn the question and negative pattern that belongs to the form you choose.

## Forms and sentence patterns

- Possession with have: I have a bicycle; she has a bicycle.
- With got: I have got a bicycle; she has got a bicycle.
- Questions: Do you have a car? Have you got a car?
- Negatives: She does not have a car. She has not got a car.

## Examples in context

> Our neighbours have two dogs.
> Have you got your ticket? Yes, I have.
> We have lunch at one o’clock.

## Common mistakes and important distinctions

Do not mix the two question patterns: Do you have a pen? or Have you got a pen?, not Do you have got a pen? For a past possession, **had** is the straightforward choice: I had a red bicycle. Had got normally has a different perfect meaning. In short answers, match the auxiliary: Yes, I do after Do you have, and Yes, I have after Have you got.
